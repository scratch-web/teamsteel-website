1Unit fractions and like denominators

Count equal-size parts of the same whole.

Watch Math Antics: What Are Fractions?Khan Academy: fraction lessons

Worked example

2/6 + 3/6 = 5/6

  1. Draw one bar split into 6 equal parts. Each part is 1/6, a unit fraction.
  2. Shade 2 parts, then shade 3 more parts. Count 5 sixths.
  3. Add the numerators: 2 + 3 = 5. Keep the denominator 6 because the size of each part has not changed.
  4. For subtraction, remove parts: 5/6 − 2/6 = 3/6 = 1/2.

Why it works: The denominator names the size of the parts. The numerator counts how many parts.

Watch out: Do not add the denominators. Sixth-size pieces stay sixth-size pieces.

Try it

3/8 + 4/8

Check your answer

7/8

2Equivalent fractions with models

Show the same amount with different-size parts.

Watch Math Antics: Simplifying FractionsKhan Academy: fraction lessons

Worked example

1/2 = 2/4 = 4/8

  1. Draw three bars of equal length; every bar represents the same-size whole.
  2. Split the first into 2 equal parts and shade 1.
  3. Split the second into 4 equal parts and shade 2. Split the third into 8 equal parts and shade 4.
  4. Line up the bars. The shaded amount is identical even though the number and size of pieces differ.

Why it works: Equivalent fractions have the same value. Smaller pieces require a larger count to cover the same amount.

Watch out: Compare models of the same-size whole. Half of a large pizza is not the same amount of food as half of a small pizza.

Try it

Complete: 2/3 = ___/6.

Check your answer

4/6. Split each third into two equal pieces.

3Multipliers, divisors, and simplifying

Rename a fraction without changing its value.

Watch Math Antics: Simplifying FractionsKhan Academy: fraction lessons

Worked example

3/5 = 12/20; 12/20 = 3/5

  1. To rename 3/5 in twentieths, find the multiplier: 5 × 4 = 20.
  2. Multiply both numbers by 4: (3 × 4)/(5 × 4) = 12/20.
  3. To simplify 12/20, divide both numbers by a common factor: (12 ÷ 4)/(20 ÷ 4) = 3/5.
  4. Check whether the new numerator and denominator share a factor greater than 1. If they do, simplify again.

Why it works: Multiplying or dividing both numbers by the same nonzero number preserves the fraction's value.

Watch out: Do not change only one number. Multiplying only the denominator changes the value.

Try it

Rename 4/7 in twenty-firsts, then simplify 18/24.

Check your answer

4/7 = 12/21; 18/24 = 3/4.

4Compare fractions

Compare amounts using the same-size whole.

Watch Math Antics: Comparing FractionsKhan Academy: fraction lessons

Worked example

3/4 > 2/3

  1. Choose a common denominator: 12 is a multiple of both 4 and 3.
  2. Rename both fractions: 3/4 = 9/12 and 2/3 = 8/12.
  3. Compare the counts of equal-size parts: 9 twelfths is greater than 8 twelfths.
  4. Write 3/4 > 2/3. A model or number line can confirm the comparison.

Why it works: Common denominators let us compare the same-size pieces. Benchmarks such as 1/2 can also help.

Watch out: A larger denominator alone does not mean a larger fraction. For unit fractions, more equal parts make each part smaller.

Try it

Compare 5/8 and 7/12.

Check your answer

5/8 = 15/24 and 7/12 = 14/24, so 5/8 > 7/12.

5Fractions greater than one

Convert between improper fractions and mixed numbers.

Watch Math Antics: Mixed NumbersKhan Academy: fraction lessons

Worked example

11/4 = 2 3/4; 2 3/4 = 11/4

  1. An improper fraction has a numerator greater than or equal to its denominator. It represents at least one whole.
  2. Group 11 fourths into wholes: 4 fourths + 4 fourths + 3 fourths.
  3. Two groups make 2 wholes, with 3/4 left. So 11/4 = 2 3/4.
  4. To go back, multiply the whole number by the denominator and add the numerator: (2 × 4 + 3)/4 = 11/4.

Why it works: A mixed number and its improper fraction name exactly the same amount.

Watch out: Keep the original denominator when converting. The pieces are still fourths.

Try it

Rename 13/5 as a mixed number and 3 2/3 as an improper fraction.

Check your answer

13/5 = 2 3/5; 3 2/3 = 11/3.

6Mixed numbers with like denominators

Add and subtract whole numbers and equal-size fraction parts.

Watch Math Antics: Adding Mixed NumbersKhan Academy: fraction lessons

Worked example

2 3/5 + 1 4/5 = 4 2/5

  1. Add the whole numbers: 2 + 1 = 3.
  2. Add the fractions: 3/5 + 4/5 = 7/5.
  3. Rename 7/5 as 1 2/5. Combine it with the 3 wholes to get 4 2/5.
  4. For subtraction with regrouping: 5 1/5 − 2 3/5. Rename 5 1/5 as 4 6/5 by exchanging 1 whole for 5/5.
  5. Subtract: 4 6/5 − 2 3/5 = 2 3/5.

Why it works: One whole can be renamed as denominator/denominator. Regrouping changes the form, not the value.

Watch out: When you exchange a whole for fraction parts, reduce the whole-number part by 1.

Try it

4 1/7 − 1 5/7

Check your answer

4 1/7 = 3 8/7; 3 8/7 − 1 5/7 = 2 3/7.

7Add fractions with unlike denominators

Rename fractions as equal-size pieces before adding.

Watch Math Antics: Common Denominator: LCDKhan Academy: addition, subtraction & practiceWatch Math Antics: adding & subtracting basics

Worked example

2/3 + 1/4 = 11/12

  1. List multiples: 3, 6, 9, 12; and 4, 8, 12. Choose 12.
  2. Rename 2/3 by multiplying both numbers by 4: 2/3 = 8/12.
  3. Rename 1/4 by multiplying both numbers by 3: 1/4 = 3/12.
  4. Add the numerators and keep the common denominator: 8/12 + 3/12 = 11/12.
  5. Simplify if possible. Eleven twelfths is already simplest. It is less than 1, which fits the original amounts.

Why it works: Thirds and fourths are different-size pieces. Twelfths give us one shared unit to count.

Watch out: Do not add straight across: 2/3 + 1/4 is not 3/7.

Try it

3/4 + 1/6

Check your answer

9/12 + 2/12 = 11/12.

8Subtract fractions with unlike denominators

Rename fractions as equal-size pieces before subtracting.

Watch Math Antics: Common Denominator: LCDKhan Academy: addition, subtraction & practiceWatch Math Antics: adding & subtracting basics

Worked example

5/6 − 1/4 = 7/12

  1. Choose a common denominator for 6 and 4: 12.
  2. Rename 5/6 = 10/12 and 1/4 = 3/12.
  3. Subtract the numerators: 10/12 − 3/12 = 7/12.
  4. Simplify if possible. Check by addition: 7/12 + 3/12 = 10/12 = 5/6.

Why it works: We can remove parts when the starting amount and the amount removed use the same-size unit.

Watch out: Keep the order of subtraction. Do not reverse the numbers just because it looks easier.

Try it

7/8 − 1/3

Check your answer

21/24 − 8/24 = 13/24.

9Add mixed numbers with unlike denominators

Combine unlike fraction parts and regroup a sum greater than one.

Watch Math Antics: Adding Mixed NumbersKhan Academy: addition, subtraction & practice

Worked example

2 3/4 + 1 2/3 = 4 5/12

  1. Rename the fraction parts with denominator 12: 3/4 = 9/12 and 2/3 = 8/12.
  2. Add the wholes: 2 + 1 = 3.
  3. Add the fraction parts: 9/12 + 8/12 = 17/12.
  4. Rename 17/12 = 1 5/12, then combine: 3 + 1 5/12 = 4 5/12.
  5. Check: both amounts are close to 3 and 2, so a sum a little below 5 is sensible.

Why it works: The fraction sum can make an extra whole. That whole belongs in the final whole-number part.

Watch out: Do not stop at 3 17/12 when the directions ask for a mixed number with a proper fraction part.

Try it

1 5/6 + 2 3/4

Check your answer

1 10/12 + 2 9/12 = 3 19/12 = 4 7/12.

10Subtract mixed numbers and regroup

Exchange a whole when the fraction part is too small to subtract.

Watch Math Antics: Subtracting Mixed NumbersKhan Academy: addition, subtraction & practice

Worked example

4 1/3 − 2 5/6 = 1 1/2

  1. Rename both fraction parts in sixths: 4 1/3 = 4 2/6.
  2. Two sixths is less than five sixths. Exchange 1 whole for 6/6: 4 2/6 = 3 8/6.
  3. Subtract the wholes and the fraction parts: 3 8/6 − 2 5/6 = 1 3/6.
  4. Simplify: 1 3/6 = 1 1/2. Check by adding 1 1/2 + 2 5/6 = 4 1/3.
  5. If starting with a whole number, rename it too: 5 − 2 3/4 = 4 4/4 − 2 3/4 = 2 1/4.

Why it works: Regrouping preserves the total: one fewer whole and denominator more fraction parts.

Watch out: Never subtract the smaller numerator from the larger one by reversing them. Regroup the starting number.

Try it

6 1/4 − 3 2/3

Check your answer

6 3/12 = 5 15/12; subtract 3 8/12 to get 2 7/12.

11Estimate and check reasonable answers

Use benchmarks to judge whether an exact answer makes sense.

Khan Academy: addition, subtraction & practice

Worked example

3 5/6 + 2 1/5 ≈ 6; exact answer: 6 1/30

  1. Use benchmarks 0, 1/2, and 1 for fraction parts. Five sixths is close to 1; one fifth is close to 0.
  2. Estimate 3 5/6 as 4 and 2 1/5 as 2. The sum should be close to 6.
  3. Find the exact sum: 3 25/30 + 2 6/30 = 5 31/30 = 6 1/30.
  4. Compare: 6 1/30 is close to 6. The exact result fits the estimate.

Why it works: An estimate is a nearby, easy-to-use value. It helps catch answers that are much too large or too small.

Watch out: An estimate checks reasonableness; it does not replace the exact answer when one is requested.

Try it

Estimate, then solve 2 7/8 + 1 1/10.

Check your answer

Estimate: 3 + 1 = 4. Exact: 3 39/40, close to 4.

12Real-world and multistep problems

Choose operations that match the story, then label and check the answer.

Khan Academy: addition, subtraction & practice

Worked example

7 − 2 3/4 + 1 1/2 = 5 3/4 liters

  1. Read the story: a container starts with 7 liters. We pour out 2 3/4 liters, then add 1 1/2 liters.
  2. Identify the question: how much is in the container now? Pouring out means subtracting; adding water means adding.
  3. First solve 7 − 2 3/4 = 4 1/4 liters.
  4. Then solve 4 1/4 + 1 1/2 = 4 1/4 + 1 2/4 = 5 3/4 liters.
  5. Estimate 7 − 3 + 2 = 6 liters. The exact answer is close. Write the answer with units and explain both steps.

Why it works: The relationships in the story determine the operation. A drawing or bar model helps track what changes.

Watch out: Do not choose an operation from one keyword alone. Ask what each quantity means and what is changing.

Try it

A ribbon is 5 1/2 feet long. Cut off 1 3/4 feet, then join on 2/3 foot. What is its new length?

Check your answer

5 1/2 − 1 3/4 = 3 3/4; 3 3/4 + 2/3 = 4 5/12 feet.

13Explain, model, and find errors

Use equations and models to justify a solution and correct a mistake.

Watch Math Antics: Adding & Subtracting FractionsKhan Academy: addition, subtraction & practice

Worked example

1/2 + 1/3 = 5/6, not 2/5

  1. Read the incorrect claim: 1/2 + 1/3 = 2/5. Name the error: the numerators and denominators were both added.
  2. Choose sixths as a shared unit: 1/2 = 3/6 and 1/3 = 2/6.
  3. Add: 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6. Draw one bar with 6 equal parts; shade 3, then 2 more.
  4. Explain: 'I renamed both fractions in sixths so the pieces were the same size. Five of the six parts are shaded.'
  5. Check reasonableness: adding a positive 1/3 to 1/2 must give more than 1/2. The incorrect 2/5 is less than 1/2.

Why it works: A complete explanation connects the strategy, the equations, and the meaning of the answer.

Watch out: A correct answer alone does not explain why the method works. Use words, a model, or both.

Try it

A student says 3 1/4 − 1 3/4 = 2 2/4. Explain the error and correct it.

Check your answer

They reversed the fraction subtraction. Regroup 3 1/4 as 2 5/4. Then 2 5/4 − 1 3/4 = 1 2/4 = 1 1/2.
